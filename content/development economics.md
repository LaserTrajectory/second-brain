# Why Development Econ (or why not)

Development economics is probably the theoretical foundation within econ that best aligns with my interests. Except, it is not purely theoretical. In fact, much of my experience with development econ at Ashoka was primarily through the applied micro side of things – RCTs, and very specific interventions studied in papers that tried to assess the microeconomic conditions under which they worked. So despite the fact that I have decided over time that I do not want to work in development economics as a field, I think its overlaps with policies and its theoretical foundations certainly merit some amount of consistent exposure to good writing and/or papers.

One such example is Professor Lant Pritchett's Substack (another one of my [[prospective mentors]]), which I only just came across. Professor Pritchett's papers were a staple in my development econ class, and so I would be remiss if I did not make a consistent effort to read his work, and to delve deeper into his papers.

In [Professor Pritchett's introductory post on Substack](https://substack.com/home/post/p-173283687), he discusses exactly what I believe to be an overlap between his subfield within development econ, and my subfield as a econ/policy student: 

> Much of development research, and policy work generally, tends to focus on “what” governments should do. This tends to ignore the question of how governments can create and sustain public organizations that actually have the capability to implement with the fidelity those actions that would be effective in accomplishing the tasks they are assigned.

--- <cite>Lant Pritchett, "Introduction to the Development Economist", September 2025 (Substack)</cite>

This links very closely to my interest in data-driven governance, and again to [[Dynamic Policy Feedback Loops]]. Another important connection point is discussed by Professor Pritchett just below:

> One of my most famous papers that was never published was “[Is India a Flailing State?](https://bsc.hks.harvard.edu/publications/is-india-a-flailing-state-detours-on-the-four-lane-highway-to-modernization/)” which points out that the Indian state can do some things impressively well, like holding elections, and even do things of impressive complexity, like send a rocket to the moon, and, yet core responsibilities like basic education, primary health, water and sanitation, and policing were mediocre (at best). This led to the collaboration with Matt Andrews and Michael Woolcock on the book "[Building State Capability](https://bsc.hks.harvard.edu/publications/building-state-capability-evidence-analysis-action/)” and an organizational approach to increasing capability called “Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation” (PDIA) for which HKS provides [on-line training](https://bsc.hks.harvard.edu/engage/training/pdia-online-course/).

--- <cite>Lant Pritchett, "Introduction to the Development Economist", September 2025 (Substack)</cite>
