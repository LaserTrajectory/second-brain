# What are Dynamic Policy Feedback Loops?

**Sunday, December 14, 2025**

This is what I hope to make the core idea of my [[thesis]]. It's essentially a system that self-corrects and adds in additional information regarding policy environments, feeding governance structures with up-to-date adjustments to what should be their next policy adjustment. We can think of it almost like a [[Monetary Policy]] system.

# A Couple of Key Ideas

Part of figuring out this idea of dynamic policy loops is trying to figure out what is the social/economic/political basis for such a system. I use all of those terms because such a system is inherently interdisciplinary – I would want a holistic consideration of systems design to come in here.

This article on [Administrative Imagination](https://substack.com/home/post/p-179080769) is really illuminating on that front. Professor Jae Yeon Kim (a fantastic professor and writer who will definitely be on my [[prospective mentors]] list) writes:

> Data-driven does not mean we must be atheoretical. Practical does not mean concepts are not useful. Abstracting a problem to a useful degree helps us design tools and solutions that address a class of problems, not just one. This is related to, but distinct from, domain expertise, which helps us identify the right problems and measure what matters. Theory helps us see that a particular tree is part of a particular forest.

--- <cite>Jae Yeon Kim, in "Administrative Imagination" (Substack)</cite>

This is a perfect articulation of my ideas regarding scale. It's important to express that part of why I want to use data science as an approach to public policy is that it has the ability to allow us to avoid scalability being the ceiling it currently is for many innovating policy interventions.

It would be ideal if I ensure I think about abstracting to a broader class of problems every time I aim to solve a particular set of policy problems.
